Introduction

Disinformation campaigns have become a persistent feature of modern media environments, exploiting existing political, social, and economic divisions to shape public opinion and undermine trust in institutions [1, 2]. While governments and researchers study and track disinformation, everyday citizens experience its effects directly through the news and social media they consume, often without the tools to recognize when they are being targeted. Understanding the characteristics of media environments associated with disinformation can help people think more critically about the information they encounter, and can foster the recognition that vulnerability to disinformation is shaped by broader media systems, not just individual false stories [1]. While addressing a lack in the general public’s understanding of disinformation campaigns, this research is also useful to media personnel and government officials interested in legislative applications of disinformation campaign detection. Specifically, we examine whether characteristics of a country’s media environment can be associated with the intensity of foreign and domestic disinformation campaigns, using large-scale news data and expert-coded disinformation indicators to develop interpretable, geographic models of disinformation risk.

Background

A good amount of research has examined how misinformation and disinformation undermine democratic institutions and erode public trust. This work frequently points to the media ecosystem itself as a key feature, arguing that the structure and dynamics of information environments, not just isolated false claims, shape how quickly and widely disinformation spreads. Benkler, Faris, and Roberts describe how partisan media ecosystems can create self-reinforcing loops that allow certain outlets and audiences to become increasingly insulated from correct information, making them more susceptible to sustained disinformation campaigns [1]. This ecosystem-level view suggests that disinformation is not a simple matter of individual bad actors producing false content, but a larger issue shaped by the broader structural features of a country’s media landscape.

Research on foreign influence operations reinforces this more broad, environment based perspective. Mazarr et al. describe how state and non-state actors engage in social manipulation campaigns, and emphasize that these campaigns are most effective when they exploit divisions and contentions that already exist within a society [2]. Common targets include political elections, economic anxieties, migration debates, public health controversies, religious tensions, and eroding trust in institutions. Instead of creating new areas of friction, these campaigns tend to amplify and weaponize existing social and political fractures, suggesting that susceptibility to disinformation is closely tied to the thematic content within a country’s media at a given time.

Despite this consistent emphasis on media environments in the theoretical literature, most empirical detection work has operated at a much narrower level of analysis. The dominant approach in the disinformation detection literature focuses on classifying individual articles, posts, or accounts as false or misleading, usually using natural language processing techniques applied to text-level features. There is still a gap between the theoretical understanding that media ecosystems matter and empirical methods that primarily operate at the individual level of content.

Recent advances in large-scale data infrastructure make it possible to address this gap. The GDELT (Global Data on Events, Location, and Tone) project provides a continuously updated, machine-coded archive of global news content, capturing tone, actors, themes, and events across a wide range of countries and languages [3]. This scale and breadth make GDELT well-suited to characterizing media environments in aggregate, rather than analyzing individual news items in isolation. Complementing this, the Digital Society Project (DSP) provides expert-coded, cross-nationally comparable measures of both foreign and domestic disinformation campaign activity, drawing on assessments from country experts rather than automated classification alone [4].

Data

This project utilizes two primary data sources. Media tone characteristics were sourced from the GDELT Project (Global Database of Events Language and Tone), which monitors broadcast, print, and web news. Organized at the article level, it lists the date and location of the media in addition to various tone, theme, and content measures. The database holds around 2.5T of information per year. GDELT has an open license and is free for the general public to use. While information spans decades, changes in collection methodology leaves data prior to 2015 unreliable for our project. 

Ratings of disinformation detection for each nation of interest were derived from measures provided by the Digital Society Project (DSP). The DSP conducts research regarding the cross section of internet and politics. Since 2000, the project has gathered annual national data across 179 countries. They do this by having a survey that asks country experts to assess how frequently different political actors use varied types of media to spread misleading or false information, and distinguishes both the actor responsible and the audience being targeted. In their primary dataset, they measure 33 expert-rated indicators of freedoms in internet and politics. We isolated indicators of targeted misinformation campaigns and recorded data from 2015 to 2025 in order to match the scope of GDELT.   

Transformation Methods

To begin media measure ingestion through the GDELT project, we first had to design a system of aggregation at the country-year level, as the database’s raw data is stored at the article level, describing trillions of observations of article features. The database is accessible only through the Google service BigQuery, which limits interapplication operability but allows for SQL query interaction and data exportation. Due to these limitations we created a multilayered table view which we transferred as a flat file.

Our final aggregated dataset included 19 summary statistics at the country-year level overall and across 11 theme groups. Summary statistics encompassed article counts both raw and relative to that observation, positive/negative tone, language polarity, language activity/passivity, group vs self language, the presence of social media embeddings, average words lengths, and shares of world length buckets (short, medium, long, very long). Theme groups included one neutral theme and 10 themes derived from previous literature regarding the relationship between mass media and disinformation campaigns. These themes were: Social Unrest, Social Identity Division, Military Conflict, Governance Trust, Information Environment, Economic Instability, Democratic Elections, Public Health, Climate Environment, Migration, and Cyber Security [2]. Articles were flagged as a particular theme according to content tags provided by GDELT. Of the thousands of possible content tags we chose 10-20 to represent each theme group in order to ensure specificity of grouping. These theme groups were non-exclusive with each article falling under 2 themes on average, a level we found appropriately representative of the varied nature of article content. GDELT querying resulted in a raw aggregate table containing 3,148 observations of 249 columns, which we then transformed into a final feature vector of 2,591 observations of 174 variables. 

To begin feature transformations we removed unuseful information which we recorded as a precaution, namely variation scores for each recorded average. Each theme’s metrics were then shrunk in comparison to the same measure of the baseline theme in that country-year. Shrinking allowed us to maintain the inherent social context of a nation or language and reduce the signal of themes with little record. Finally, measures were logged (or Center Log Ratioed) to account for the major skew in the data. 

Of the 33 variables selected from the DSP, we chose 4 which were averaged to create a domestic-sourced and a foreign-sourced dissemination score variable respectively. The foreign score combines two DSP indicators: the frequency that foreign governments and their agents use social media to spread misleading information about a country’s domestic politics, and the frequency that they do through paid advertisements. The domestic score similarly combines two indicators: how often a country’s own government disseminates false information to influence its population, and how often major political parties and candidates do the same. Together, these capture disinformation spread through official state networks as well as partisan political organizations, both of which originate within the country and not from a foreign actor.

Analysis

Predictive Modeling was accomplished using Random Forest and XGBoost models. Linear Modeling was used to explore both dimensionality reduction and prediction, but was ultimately ineffective. Random Forest modeling was first conducted in order to reduce dimensions and rerun on the smaller feature vector as a predictive tool using both country-groupings and time series analysis. Concerns of limited complexity led us to also run a XGBoost model with similar constraints.

Conclusions

Across our preliminary results, domestic disinformation campaigns proved easier to predict and interpret than foreign campaigns. This difference likely reflects underlying differences in how domestic and foreign disinformation operate within a media ecosystem. Domestic campaigns are typically embedded in existing political and media institutions, drawing on familiar rhetorical patterns, recurring political actors, and locally recurring issues and consistently in national news coverage. Foreign campaigns, by contrast, are often designed to obscure their origin and adapt tactically to local contexts, which may introduce more heterogeneity in how they manifest across countries and over time. This is consistent with prior work suggesting that foreign influence operations succeed in part by copying domestic voices and blending into existing media environments, which would naturally make them harder to distinguish using aggregate media characteristics alone. This suggests that models built for domestic disinformation detection may generalize well across time within a country, while models targeting foreign campaigns may require more country-specific tuning or richer features to reach comparable predictive performance.

Several features that we expected to be strong predictors based on prior literature did not emerge as important in our models. Largely, sentiment polarity, a commonly used feature in misinformation detection research, showed limited predictive value in our results. One possible explanation is that polarity operates is not able to distinguish disinformation-associated content from ordinary politically charged reporting. Emotionally charged tone is common in real news coverage that covers contentious issues, which may weaken polarity’s signal when aggregated to the country-year level. Similarly, social media embeddings did not contribute meaningfully to our models. This may reflect that social media dynamics operate on shorter timescales and through different mechanisms than the broader news media patterns captured by GDELT, meaning that meaningful signals in social media data may be too weak when aggregated annually alongside traditional news content. These results suggest that feature engineering approaches drawn from article level detection tasks do not necessarily transfer well to environment level analysis, and that different theoretical mechanisms may be at play at each level of analysis.

In contrast, features capturing thematic content, particularly the relative prevalence of themes such as military conflict, identity division, election democracy, and information environment showed stronger association with disinformation intensity. This pattern is consistent with theoretical accounts suggesting that disinformation campaigns succeed by exploiting existing social and political fault lines rather than manufacturing entirely new narratives. We interpret this as evidence that the fault lines are actively being discussed within a country, providing a more direct signal of vulnerability to disinformation than tone or sentiment alone.

Notably, our models are cross-nationally predictive and do not take regional differences into account. Patterns are reflective of global consistency while much literature is specific to a nation of interest. Capturing specific patterns would require more complex modeling and greater observations to support such work.

Limitations

This study has several limitations related to data sources. First, GDELT’s underlying news coverage is known to have some geographically imbalance, with substantially more coverage of Western, English language media relative to non Western sources. This imbalance likely affects the representativeness of our media characteristics for countries with less GDELT coverage, and may partially explain weaker or noisier model performance in underrepresented regions. Second, our thematic categories, while interpretable, were relatively narrow and specific, which may have limited the model’s ability to capture broader disinformation narratives that do not map cleanly onto a single theme that we chose. Third, our outcome measure relies on a single expert-coded source, the Digital Society Project. While DSP offers valuable cross-national comparability, expert-coded measures can carry annotation biases from the pool of country experts consulted, and our analysis would likely benefit from a second, independent expert-rating source to help validate DSP’s disinformation intensity scores.

A second category of limitations relates to computational constraints. Our GDELT queries were executed through Google BigQuery, and both query volume and processing time were constrained by budget and time limitations, meaning our analysis draws on a subset of available GDELT data rather than its full historical and geographic coverage. Similarly, once data was retrieved, our ability to store, transform, and iterate on features was limited by available computing infrastructure and the overall time frame of this project. These constraints limited both the granularity of features we could construct and the extent of experimentation possible during model development. Future work with greater computational resources and time could expand both the temporal and geographic scope of the analysis, and support more extensive feature engineering, particularly around thematic granularity, which may reveal relationships not detectable at the scale of the current study. Further research should also include an addition of a neural network, as a more complex model would be needed to accurately predict how much dissemination a country would experience.

Our project results are modest but provide a strong preliminary framework on which to build upon. In scope, measure, and analysis, our methods are broad and by extension cannot encapsulate more complex patterns.
